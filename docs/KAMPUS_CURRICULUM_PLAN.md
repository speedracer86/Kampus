# Kampus: Curriculum & Engagement Plan

*Grades 3–8 lesson map, real-world wraps, and reward-economy design — grounded in the research, checked against what's actually in the app today.*

---

## 1. What the research says (short version)

**Which lessons matter most.** Common Core defines a "major work of the grade" for every grade — the clusters where students should spend 65–85% of their time (Achieve the Core / Student Achievement Partners). This is the best available answer to "what are the best lessons per grade": teach the major work deeply, use everything else in service of it. The per-grade maps below are built on those documents.

**How the best programs structure a lesson.** Zearn — one of the few digital math programs with large randomized-trial evidence behind it — structures every lesson the same way: a short fluency warm-up, then concept learning with pictures and objects, then problem solving with visual models, moving concrete → pictorial → abstract, with adaptive support that keeps kids in grade-level work instead of demoting them. Kampus already does most of this (adaptive tiers that sprinkle easier wins rather than demote, number-line and fraction-bar visuals, one-step teach reveals). What Kampus is missing is the *warm-up ramp* and, more importantly, **context**: most of the current generators are bare computation ("9 × (−9)"), and the evidence (IES middle-school engagement work, contextualized-task research) is consistent that anchoring problems in situations kids recognize — prices, discounts, temperatures, recipes, allowances — is one of the strongest engagement levers available.

**What makes rewards work — and the trap to avoid.** Your instinct (wrap teaching around the real world, Kredits → real rewards through parents) is well supported, with one critical refinement. Token economies are one of the best-evidenced behavioral tools for ADHD specifically — immediate, visible, exchangeable points are exactly what an under-responsive reward system needs. But Fryer's randomized trials across four cities (~$6.3M in incentives, 38,000 students) found something sharp: **paying for inputs (reading a book, finishing assignments, attendance) raised achievement; paying for outputs (test scores, grades) did essentially nothing.** Kids don't know how to convert "I want the reward" into a higher test score, so output rewards just create anxiety. And the ADHD-specific guidance (including Monster Math's own reward-design piece) adds: rewards must be proactive and predictable in *structure* (never negotiated in the moment), focused on effort and progress, mixed with social and mystery rewards, co-designed with the kid, and gradually faded as habits form.

**The implication for Kampus:** pay tickets for *showing up and doing the work* — sessions completed, teach-steps studied, second-looks fixed, streaks of days — not for accuracy or test scores. Celebrate test passes loudly (confetti, grade unlock, a badge) but don't put the money there.

**Bottom line on coverage:** grades 6–7 are in good shape; grade 4 has a real hole (decimals); grade 5 is missing decimal division and volume; grade 8 is missing half its major work (functions, systems); and the whole app under-uses word-problem framing — which is exactly the "wrap it in the real world" change you're proposing. Geometry and statistics are absent as strands, but they don't need a fifth world: area/volume fold into Fraction Factory (recipes → trays → boxes), stats and probability fold into the Lemonade Stand, Pythagorean into Fraction Factory's "build" identity.

## 3. Making it fun: the design moves, each tied to evidence

**Context-first questions.** Rewrite generators so the *default* presentation is a one-sentence real-world story with the bare computation available as the "just the math" view (a toggle — some ADHD kids find word problems higher-friction on low-focus days, and the research supports reducing reading load when the goal is fluency). Every wrap in the tables above uses money, food, phones, games, or weather — things a 13-year-old actually touches. This is the IES engagement finding operationalized.

**The Lemonade Stand becomes a curriculum vehicle, not a minigame.** It already generates real data (prices, weather, demand, profit). Grade-gate what it asks of her: G5 unit prices, G6 averages of her own sales history, G7 probability of rain and percent profit margins, G8 profit-as-a-function-of-price. Math about *her own business decisions* is the strongest real-world wrap available — nothing is more relevant than data you generated yourself.

**Concrete → pictorial → abstract everywhere (the Zearn spine).** The number-line taps and fraction bars already exist; extend them to G3 fractions-on-a-number-line, G3 area grids (tap the brownies), G5 volume (stacked-cube visual), G8 slope (a mini savings graph). Tier 1 of every skill should have a visual, tappable representation; tier 3 goes abstract.

**A 90-second fluency warm-up to open each run.** Three quick below-tier questions before the real set. Zearn opens every lesson this way; for ADHD it doubles as an on-ramp — immediate early wins before the harder material, which the app's "sprinkle easier wins" logic already half-implements.

**Mystery and social rewards on top of the token economy.** Two cheap, high-leverage additions from the ADHD reward literature: an occasional mystery bonus (a sealed "?" that opens after a run — sometimes 2× Kredits, sometimes a pet cameo, sometimes nothing but confetti; novelty is its own reward for ADHD brains, and *variable* reward schedules sustain engagement better than fixed ones), and a parent-side "send a high-five" button on the Grown-ups screen that pops a note on her next session — social reward, which the evidence says ADHD kids respond to disproportionately.

**Let her set the goal.** Self-monitoring and co-created goals build the internal accountability that pure external rewards can't. A tiny weekly ritual: "Pick this week's target — pass a tier? 4 practice days? Fix 10 second-looks?" Her choice, visible on the home screen, ticket bonus on completion. This is also your fade-out path: over months, the goals she sets become the motivation, and the money matters less.

## 4. The reward economy: your instinct, refined

Your model — Kredits in-app, parents converting effort into real-world rewards — is a token economy, and token economies are among the best-evidenced tools for ADHD motivation. Keep it. Three refinements from the evidence:

**Pay for inputs, never outputs.** This is the Fryer finding and it should be the constitution of the ticket economy. Tickets for: completing a practice run (any score), a teach-step actually read/listened to, second-look fixes attempted, days-practiced streaks, her self-set weekly goal. No tickets for: accuracy percentages, test scores, or beating a sibling/benchmark. The current app already leans this way (milestone tickets for finishing runs) but also pays 5 tickets for passing a grade test — that's an output. Recommendation: keep the test-pass *celebration* enormous (it unlocks a whole grade — that's the reward) but move the ticket value into the practice that led to it.

**Rethink "Teach me costs half."** Right now asking to be taught halves that question's Kredits. The logic was "knowing it yourself pays more," but under the inputs lens, studying a worked explanation is precisely the input we want to reward — and for an ADHD kid who already finds help-seeking effortful, a financial penalty teaches help-avoidance. Recommendation: first teach-view on any new skill pays a small *bonus* ("Scholar bonus +5"); repeat teach-views on the same question are simply neutral. The Kredit economy stays balanced because the bonus is small and once-per-skill.

**Keep the cap, keep parents as the mint, plan the fade.** The ~$5/week cap is right — the literature warns against payout-per-behavior escalation ("expecting a payout each time"). Parents defining the reward menu keeps rewards tied to the family's actual life (screen time, movie pick, an outing) rather than cash-for-grades. And build the fade into the product from day one: as streaks stabilize, the parent dashboard should nudge the ratio toward social/experience rewards and self-set goals — the research is clear that a good token economy is scaffolding you eventually remove, not a permanent salary.

## 5. Build order (what I'd implement, in priority)

1. **Grade 4 decimals + Grade 5 decimal division** — a real curriculum hole, and money context makes it the easiest build (highest correctness-per-effort).
2. **Context wraps on existing generators** — biggest engagement win, no new math needed; start with Trade Tycoon and Weather Station where stories are natural.
3. **Grade 7 percent applications (tax/tip/discount/interest)** — most life-relevant gap, fits Trade Tycoon perfectly.
4. **Ticket-economy rework** — inputs-only tickets, Scholar bonus replacing the teach penalty, self-set weekly goal. Small code, big behavioral stakes.
5. **Lemonade Stand curriculum hooks** — G6 stats and G7 probability from her own sales data.
6. **Grade 8 functions + systems** — needed before she gets there; some runway if she's entering grade 7 material.
7. **Geometry folds** (G3 area, G5 volume, G8 Pythagorean) + fluency warm-up + mystery/social rewards.
